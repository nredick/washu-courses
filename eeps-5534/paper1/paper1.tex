Seismic anisotropy is a phenomenon where seismic waves travel at different speeds depending on their direction of propagation \parencite{savageSeismicAnisotropyMantle1999}. It is commonly accepted that seismic anisotropy results from the lattice-preferred orientation (LPO) of anisotropic minerals in the mantle \parencite{hansenReviewMechanismsGenerating2021,babuskaSeismicAnisotropyEarth1991}, an idea that was originally proposed by \textcite{hessSeismicAnisotropyUppermost1964}. The formation of seismic anisotropy is largely controlled by the local deformation regime, which in turn depends on factors such as temperature, pressure, or the presence of water \parencite{hansenReviewMechanismsGenerating2021,skemerInfluenceWaterLPO2013}. As such, seismic anisotropy is a valuable tool for understanding deformational processes, structure, and physical conditions of the Earth's interior.
% todo longMantleDynamicsSeismic2010
However, studies of seismic anisotropy often assume that the lower mantle is largely isotropic \parencite{silverSeismicAnisotropyContinents1996,savageSeismicAnisotropyMantle1999}---an assumption based largely on laboratory experiments of peridotites demonstrating that LPO does not develop during diffusion creep \parencite{meadeLaboratorySeismologicalObservations1995,zhangLatticePreferredOrientation1995,karatoRoleRecrystallizationPreferred1988}, which is the dominant deformation regime expected under lower mantle conditions. However, convective flow in the lower 300~km of the mantle, known as the D$''$ layer after \textcite{bullenEarthModelBased2013}, has been shown to drive LPO development \parencite{karatoGeodynamicSignificanceSeismic2008}. Seismic anisotropy in the D$''$ layer has important implications for how we interpret observations of seismic anisotropy and our understanding of CMB dynamics.
% but recent research has revealed that this assumption may not be accurate \parencite{...}. In fact, several studies have reported the presence of anisotropy in the lower mantle, which has significant implications for our understanding of the Earth's interior.
We typically measure anisotropy using shear wave splitting (SWS), which can constrain the orientation and magnitude of anisotropy \parencite{chevrotShearWaveSplitting2004,longShearWaveSplitting2009}. New techniques for identifying and isolating deep mantle seismic anisotropy, such as differential splitting and waveform inversions \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}, can provide important insights into the dynamics of the lower mantle.
However, adequately constraining the source location---particularly the depth---of anisotropy remains an outstanding challenge in geophysics \parencite{fischerDepthDistributionMantle1996,liuEstimationDepthAnisotropy2011}, primarily because measurements of seismic anisotropy are integrated along the full receiver-side ray-path of the wave \parencite{longShearWaveSplitting2009}.
Our understanding of seismic anisotropy along the core-mantle boundary (CMB) is further complicated by the relationship between anisotropy and large low-shear velocity provinces (LLSVPs) \parencite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017,wolfGlobalCompilationDeep2023}, ultra-low velocity zones (ULVZs) \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}, and the post-perovskite phase transition \parencite{peltierMantleDynamicsLayer2007}.
% The CMB is a region of intense geological activity, and the presence of anisotropy in this region may be related to the complex dynamics and interactions between the Earth's core and mantle.
Integrating multidisciplinary techniques is essential for advancing our understanding of seismic anisotropy in the deep mantle, as it allows us to relate the observed seismic properties to the underlying mineralogical and compositional characteristics of the Earth's interior. This paper will review our contemporary understanding CMB anisotropy through a multidisciplinary lens that combines seismology, mineral physics, and geochemistry to discuss how we measure and interpret deep mantle anisotropy, and will outline outstanding questions and challenges.
