Seismic anisotropy is a phenomenon in which seismic waves travel at different speeds depending on their propagation direction. It is commonly accepted that seismic anisotropy results primarily from the lattice-preferred orientation (LPO) formed during the deformation of anisotropic minerals in the mantle. As such, seismic anisotropy is a valuable tool for understanding deformational processes, structure, and the physical conditions of the Earth's interior.
Studies of seismic anisotropy often assume the bulk lower mantle is isotropic; however, there are several lines of evidence supporting seismic anisotropy from both LPO and shape-preferred orientation (SPO) in the lowermost 200--300~km of the mantle, known as the D$''$.
We typically measure anisotropy using shear wave splitting (SWS), which can constrain the orientation and magnitude of anisotropy. Techniques for identifying and isolating deep mantle seismic anisotropy, such as differential splitting and waveform inversions, can provide important insights into the dynamics of the lower mantle.
% However, adequately constraining the source---particularly the depth---of anisotropy remains an outstanding challenge in geophysics.
This paper will review our contemporary understanding of anisotropy in the D$''$ along the core-mantle boundary (CMB) through a multidisciplinary lens combining mineral physics, geochemistry, and seismology to discuss how we measure and interpret deep mantle anisotropy, and outline outstanding questions and challenges.

In the lowermost mantle, the 200--300~km above the core-mantle boundary (CMB) is characterized by changes in temperature, seismic velocity, partial melt, and chemical heterogeneity, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:anomalies_dprimeprime}. This layer, known as the D$''$ layer after \textcite{bullenCompressibilityPressureHypothesisEarths1949}, is the transition between the outer core and the lower mantle. It has a distinct and variable topography, with significant lateral variations in the observed characteristics \parencite{garneroNewParadigmEarths2004}.
\begin{wrapfigure}{l}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[height=0.45\textheight]{anomalies_dprimeprime}
    \caption{Changes in temperature (a), seismic velocity (b), partial melt (c), and chemical heterogeneity (d) between the lower mantle and the D$''$ layer, adapted from \textcite{peltierMantleDynamicsLayer2007}.}
    \label{fig:anomalies_dprimeprime}
\end{wrapfigure}
The D$''$ layer plays a fundamental role in the dynamics of the overlying mantle and the core below; it is the lower thermal boundary layer of the mantle convection system, it controls the heat flux out of the core that affects the geodynamo, and it is critical for understanding mantle plumes, slab stagnation, and global convection patterns \parencite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017,garneroNewParadigmEarths2004}. Seismic anisotropy in the D$''$ layer is a crucial tool for understanding these lowermost mantle processes and structures \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}.
However, isolating and interpreting D$''$ anisotropy poses many challenges; our understanding of seismic anisotropy in this region along the CMB is complicated by the relationship between anisotropy and large low-shear-velocity (LLVPs) \parencite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017,wolfGlobalCompilationDeep2023}, ultra-low velocity zones (ULVZs) \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}, and the distribution and contributions to anisotropy of the dominant mineral phases (post-perovskite, ferropericlase, and bridgmanite) \parencite{peltierMantleDynamicsLayer2007}.

D$''$ anisotropy is highly dependent on the distribution of the dominant lower mantle minerals and their relative anisotropies. \textcite{hirose205MineralogyDeep2015} suggest the relative abundance of the primary minerals is about \textasciitilde30\% \ce{(Mg, Fe)O} (ferropericlase; Fp) and \textasciitilde70\% \ce{(Mg, Fe)SiO3}, "…which may be present as bridgmanite [Br] in hotter regions and post-perovskite [pPv] in colder ones" \parencite{hirose205MineralogyDeep2015}. Critically, improvements in experimental technologies and techniques in the early 2000s made it possible to experimentally isolate the pPv phase, which has a strong intrinsic elastic anisotropy and may be the dominant phase in the anisotropic regions of D$''$  \parencite{oganovTheoreticalExperimentalEvidence2004,murakamiPostPerovskitePhaseTransition2004}. Phase transitions to pPv structure at lowermost mantle P-T conditions are reported for many compositions \parencite{hirosePostperovskitePhaseTransition2006}.
Generally speaking, two distinct phenomena are responsible for generating seismic anisotropy: lattice-preferred orientation (LPO) and shape-preferred orientation (SPO). LPO, which is considered the dominant source of anisotropy \parencite{savageSeismicAnisotropyMantle1999}, forms in high-strain regions as material is deformed under shear stress imposed by mantle flow. Under simple shear, deformation via dislocation creep drives the preferential realignment of the crystal axes relative to the shear direction \parencite{kocksTextureAnisotropyPreferred2000}. SPO may be an alternative or complementary explanation for D$''$ anisotropy, but it is difficult to differentiate its effect \parencite{reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019}.
% Different seismological techniques are used to infer D$''$ anisotropy, and each are able to resolve different anisotropic geometries and spatial distributions (see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}).

A variety of body wave methods are used to detect deep mantle anisotropy. Methods for D$''$ anisotropy typically rely on polarization-dependent shear waves, rather than compressional (P) waves. Each approach is sensitive to different aspects of D$''$ anisotropic geometry (i.e., radial vs. azimuthal) \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}, and when used in combination, can provide a more holistic picture of D$''$ deformation and potential structures \parencite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017}. Integrating these multidisciplinary techniques is key to advance our understanding of seismic anisotropy in the deep mantle, as it allows us to relate the underlying structural and compositional characteristics of the Earth's interior to the observed seismic properties.

\section{Mechanisms and Mineral Physics}

    \centering
    \caption{Table describing basic intrinsic properties of dominant deep mantle mineral phases, including their measured P-wave velocities, as summarized in \textcite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017}.}

LPO development in the lowermost mantle depends on the relationship between the intrinsic properties of the deforming mineral phase and the local conditions, which controls slip system activation \parencite{hansenReviewMechanismsGenerating2021,carrezImplicationsPlasticFlow2007,romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017}.
The dominant slip system in pPv is not well-constrained \parencite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017,carrezImplicationsPlasticFlow2007,reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019}. Experimental and computational studies propose different deformation mechanisms, including slip on the (001), (010) \parencite[strong \ce{CaIrO3} pPv LPO][]{carrezImplicationsPlasticFlow2007}, and \{110\} planes \parencite[\ce{MgGeO3} pPv analogue][]{carrezImplicationsPlasticFlow2007}. Because each proposed slip system produces a distinct LPO, the resulting radial and azimuthal anisotropy also differs. Recent models of LPO evolution in pPv (\textasciitilde70\%) and Fp (\textasciitilde30\%) for both CMB-parallel flow and upwelling/downwelling flow by \textcite{tommasiDeformationCrystalPreferred2018} produce both radial and azimuthal anisotropy components.

%In particular, the pPv phase transition
Fp likely also contributes to D$''$ anisotropy because of its strong intrinsic elastic anisotropy \parencite{marquardtElasticShearAnisotropy2009}. In particular, LPO developed in Fp within cold downwelling regions (e.g., sinking paleoslab material) may produce measurable SWS, although its deformation behavior and slip-system activity remain poorly constrained; however, it is less abundant than pPv \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}. The relative distribution of both phases in different regions of the lowermost mantle controls the type and strength of observed anisotropy.

SPO may also contribute to anisotropy in D$''$, but its relative importance in the lowermost mantle is debated because it is unknown whether there is sufficient melt and wetting for SPO-induced anisotropy \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}. Potential SPO anisotropic sources include alignment of partial melt lenses of pPv, compositional heterogeneity (layering), or distinct mineral domains, which can produce seismic anisotropy if the aligned structures have a sufficient elastic contrast and consistent orientation.
Interpreting anisotropy in the lowermost mantle is complicated because the distribution of these constituents is poorly constrained; pPv is unlikely to be ubiquitous across the D$''$ region. Moreover, because multiple mechanisms can produce similar radial or azimuthal anisotropy signatures, seismic observations alone rarely uniquely identify the underlying mineral fabric, deformation mechanism, or flow geometry. Interpreting D$''$ anisotropy therefore requires combining seismic observations with mineral-physics constraints and models of lowermost mantle flow.

\section{Methods for Detecting Deep Mantle Anisotropy}
% As such, I will review the most common S wave methods below.

\subsection{Differential SV-SH Travel Times}
\label{sec:diff-sv-sh}

Differential SV-SH travel times from teleseismic S, ScS, and S\textsubscript{diff} phases are among the most common methods for interrogating anisotropy in D$''$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}a) \parencite{nowackiNewAdvancesUsing2011,wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}. This method decomposes a single phase into its SH and SV polarizations, where SV particle motion lies within the great-circle plane containing the ray path and receiver, and SH particle motion is orthogonal to that plane. In a radially anisotropic medium, these two polarizations propagate at different speeds. Radial anisotropy is commonly quantified as

\begin{equation}
    \xi = \left(\frac{V_{SH}}{V_{SV}}\right)^2 = \frac{N}{L}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $N$ and $L$ are the Love parameters \parencite{skemerInferringUppermantleFlow2016}. The relative velocities of the two polarizations provide information about the orientation of the anisotropic symmetry axis: $\xi > 1$ indicates a vertically transversely isotropic (VTI) fabric with a vertical symmetry axis, and $\xi < 1$ indicates a horizontally transversely isotropic (HTI) fabric with a horizontal symmetry axis. $\xi = 1$ indicates no radial anisotropy for the sampled propagation direction, although it does not preclude azimuthal anisotropy (described in more detail in Section~\ref{sec:diff-split}) or a tilted transverse isotropy (TTI). D$''$ anisotropy is frequently interpreted as being radially anisotropic, with vertical fabrics \parencite{wysessionLateralVariationsCompressional1999,kendallConstraintsSeismicAnisotropy1996}. See Section~\ref{sec:interp} for further discussion of the causes of these theorized vertical fabrics.

\subsection{Differential Splitting Techniques}
\label{sec:diff-split}

When shear waves enter an anisotropic medium, they are split into two orthogonally polarized quasi-shear wave components that travel along the fast and slow axes of the material \parencite{silverShearWaveSplitting1991,vecseyShearwaveSplittingMeasurements2008}. The delay time ($\delta t$) between the arrival of the fast and slow components at the receiver, and the fast polarization direction ($\phi$, measured from north; or $\phi'$, in the ray frame) parameters are used to describe this shear wave splitting (SWS) phenomena (see Figure~\ref{fig:sks-summary}) \parencite{vinnikAzimuthalAnisotropyLithosphere1989,silverShearWaveSplitting1991}.
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{sks-summary}
    \caption{Schematic of SWS of SKS phase in the anisotropic D$''$ layer. Upon entering the D$''$ layer, the P wave is converted to an SKS wave phase with orthogonally polarized quasi-shear wave components, separated by a delay time ($\delta t$). The components are travelling along the fast (blue) and slow (red) axes defined by the elastic properties (e.g., LPO, SPO; here unspecified) of the D$''$ layer. While not explicitly depicted in the figure, the fast direction ($\phi$) is typically measured from north. Borrowed from \textcite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}.}
    \label{fig:sks-summary}
\end{figure}
These differential splitting techniques primarily identify azimuthal anisotropy, which assumes a horizontal symmetry axis such that velocities vary as a function of the horizontal direction \parencite{nowackiNewAdvancesUsing2011,longShearWaveSplitting2009}.
Azimuthal anisotropy can be used to distinguish horizontal flow patterns. In the Earth, SWS is commonly observed using XKS phases (SKS, SKKS, S3KS, and PKS), which undergo P-to-S conversion at the CMB before propagating upward through anisotropic mantle layers. Because this conversion produces an initially radially polarized S wave, any splitting accumulated on the source side of the path is removed; therefore, anisotropy along the receiver-side path transfers energy from the radial to the transverse component \parencite{schulte-pelkumSynthesisSeismicAnisotropy2003,silverShearWaveSplitting1991,longShearWaveSplitting2009}.
However, because the SWS effect is integrated over the whole ray path, determining where at depth anisotropy originates is a challenge.

To overcome this issue, some studies have used differential splitting between XKS phases \parencite[e.g., SKS--SKKS;][]{niuSeismicAnisotropyLower2004,vanacoreCharacterizationGalapagosIslands2011,royUpperLowerMantle2014} to  isolate the anisotropy in the deep mantle (see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}c). This method is based on the assumption that the ray paths of the phases are distinct in the D$''$ layer but nearly overlapping in the anisotropic upper mantle, therefore, large differences in the observed SWS for each phase can be attributed to anisotropy in the lowermost mantle \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024,nowackiNewAdvancesUsing2011,wolfGlobalCompilationDeep2023}. Note, however, that differences in upper mantle structure may account for small changes in the SKS-SKKS differential \parencite{linFullwaveEffectsShear2014}. Furthermore, evidence for SKS-SKKS splitting in D$''$ is rare, which is likely a result of finite-frequency effects; based on anisotropic sensitivity kernels, SKS/SKKS have a strong sensitivity kernel for a 50--200~km conic region of the upper mantle, but a weak sensitivity distributed over a 600--1000~km wide zone in the lowermost mantle \parencite{tommasiDeformationCrystalPreferred2018,sieminskiFiniteFrequencySKSSplitting2008,favierSensitivityKernelsShear2003}. Taking their limitations into account, XKS differential splitting methods are still useful for interpreting flow and anisotropic structure along LLVPs, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:african-LLVP} \parencite{reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019}.

Non-XKS phases can also be used; for instance, S-ScS differential splitting (see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}d) is a common approach based on the fact that only the ScS phase passes through the D$''$ layer, but both phases have similar ray paths in the upper mantle on both the source and receiver sides \parencite{wookeyLowermostMantleAnisotropy2005}. However, it has been shown the S-ScS differential splitting may produce apparent splitting if the phase shift of the radial component due to the CMB reflection is not taken into account \parencite{wolfConstrainingDeepMantle2022}.

\subsection{Polarity Analysis}

% Measurements of polarities of S phases that turn in the lowermost mantle (S, SdS, PdP)

Anomalous polarities of S waves reflecting off the CMB can be used to infer anisotropic structures, generally with a tilted symmetry axis, relative to vertical (TTI) \parencite{garneroVariableAzimuthalAnisotropy2004}. P and S waves reflecting off the D$''$ (SdS, PdP; see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}b) can also be used in polarity analysis using array processing techniques such as beamforming \parencite{piscontiDiscriminatingCausesAnisotropy2019,piscontiMineralogyFabricDeformation2023}. The reflection polarities are compared to other seismic phases (S/ScS and P/PcP), and can be used to differentiate between different causes for the observed anisotropy, such as phase transitions (e.g., the perovskite to post-perovskite phase transition), mineral texture or thermal anomalies \parencite{thomasAnisotropyCausePolarity2011}. The polarities are best utilized as an additional constraint for deep mantle anisotropy, rather than an independent observation \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}. \textcite{piscontiDiscriminatingCausesAnisotropy2019,piscontiMineralogyFabricDeformation2023} describe several important best practices for polarity analysis, which are well-summarized in \textcite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}.

\subsection{Anisotropic Inversions}

Past studies have computed both regional and global inversions for radially anisotropic lowermost mantle structure \parencite{frenchWholemantleRadiallyAnisotropic2014,aspletInversionShearWave2023}. ScS waves are commonly used because they have a long ray path through D$''$, and therefore can sample sufficient anisotropy \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}. However, because the observations are path-integrated, this wave inherently smooths any sharply anisotropic structures in favor of an average over the D$''$ region, and may also include upper mantle anisotropy beneath the receiver.
These studies do not typically consider variable anisotropic geometries, which makes it difficult to compare these inversions to SWS measurements of azimuthal anisotropy or TTI \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}. Joint inversion of ScS, SKS, and SKKS waveforms can address this issue by resolving more general anisotropic geometries, but distinguishing among candidate mechanisms requires dense back-azimuthal coverage (which the D$''$ layer generally does not have) and can remain non-unique even with waveform modeling \parencite{aspletInversionShearWave2023}. In regional studies, ScS inversions have been used to infer likely mantle flow directions beneath the northeastern Pacific Ocean \parencite{aspletInversionShearWave2023}, and maps of radial anisotropy for the Pacific Ocean \parencite{kawaiWaveformInversionLocalized2010,suzukiImagingPaleoslabsInferring2021}. In the central Pacific, these inversions find $\xi > 1$, interpreted as upwelling flow along the edge of the Pacific LLVP \parencite{kawaiWaveformInversionLocalized2010}.

Global anisotropic inversions also invert for radial anisotropy using both travel-time tomography and full waveform inversion techniques \parencite{frenchWholemantleRadiallyAnisotropic2014}. While the global models cannot resolve the same spatial detail as the regional inversions, they largely show the same pattern of $\xi > 1$ outside LLVPs, and $\xi > 1$ within LLVPs, suggesting upwelling flow along LLVP margins and horizontal flow within LLVPs \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}. Overall, these global anisotropic models are limited by their resolution, which make them difficult to compare with SWS studies, and the assumption of a simple radially anisotropic structure.

\subsection{S\textsubscript{diff,pol} splitting}

S\textsubscript{diff} waves are shear waves that are refracted off the CMB, and have long, horizontal ray paths through the D$''$ layer. Consequently, they are likely strongly influenced by anisotropy in this region, and can be used to infer anisotropic structure of D$''$ \parencite{wolfMeasurementSdiffSplitting2023}. Better yet, there is good global coverage of the CMB by S\textsubscript{diff} waves, which may help mitigate issues from some methods discussed above, which can have more limited sampling ranges \parencite{laiTravelTimeWaveform2020}. However, using the standard single-phase differential travel time splitting approach, as described in Section~\ref{sec:diff-sv-sh}, is not well-suited for S\textsubscript{diff} because the waves are generally not perfectly SV or SH polarized in isotropic mediums, and SV\textsubscript{diff}-SH\textsubscript{diff} can accumulate a delay time even in isotropic mediums that can be misinterpreted as SWS \parencite{komatitschSHdiffSVdiffSplittingIsotropic2010}. \textcite{wolfMeasurementSdiffSplitting2023} propose a methodology to mitigate some of these challenges by considering the initial polarization of S\textsubscript{diff}, referred to as S\textsubscript{diff,pol} splitting (see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}f). Like the methods described previously, S\textsubscript{diff,pol} splitting is still limited in its ability to constrain the source location of the anisotropy within the D$''$ layer, because the result is path-integrated and subject to a broad sensitivity kernel in this region \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024,favierSensitivityKernelsShear2003}. As such, applications of this method should consider S\textsubscript{diff} as a function of distance, or combined with a method that has better lateral resolution (e.g., XKS splitting, as described in Section~\ref{sec:diff-split}) \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024,wolfMeasurementSdiffSplitting2023}. Application of S\textsubscript{diff,pol} splitting for global inversion, and/or joint inversion with other methods is a promising new way to investigate D$''$ anisotropy.

\section{Current Interpretations of Deep Mantle Anisotropy}
\label{sec:interp}

\subsection{LLVPs and Mantle Flow}

Along the CMB, two large (reaching >1000~km above CMB) low-shear-velocity provinces (LLVPs) underlie Africa and the Pacific. Possible explanations include the accumulation of subducted paleoslab material, primordial reservoirs, and thermochemical piles in which thermal buoyancy partly offsets a compositional density increase \parencite{lithgow-bertelloniDynamicsCenozoicMesozoic1998,davailleDeflatingLLSVPsBundles2020,royLowermostMantleFlow2025}. Seismic, geochemical, and mineral physics constraints indicate that LLVPs are hotter and chemically distinct from the surrounding lowermost mantle, and that their density is higher, suggesting they are dynamically stable and long-lived \parencite{garneroContinentsizedAnomalousZones2016}. An active interpretation of LLVP origin is based on the idea that thermochemical heterogeneity generates buoyancy-driven vertical flow \parencite{davailleSimultaneousGenerationHotspots1999}; whereas the passive interpretation proposes that dense, chemically-distinct material is shaped by large-scale mantle convection, with implications for slab accumulation, CMB heat flux, and upper-mantle dynamics \parencite{mcnamaraThermochemicalStructuresAfrica2005,reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019}.

Regional seismic studies commonly report strong radial anisotropy (\textasciitilde4\%) near LLVP margins \parencite{reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019,cottaarObservationsChangingAnisotropy2013,wangMappingGeometryGeographic2004}. This has been interpreted as evidence for vertical flow, or for ambient mantle flow deflected upward along the steep sides of the provinces, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:african-LLVP} \parencite{royLowermostMantleFlow2025,romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017}. By contrast, anisotropy appears weaker and more heterogeneous in LLVP interiors, although it is not necessarily absent \parencite{cottaarObservationsChangingAnisotropy2013}. Thus, LLVP margins may drive deformation and upwelling, but their interiors may be less deformed (lower anisotropy) or less well-resolved seismically.

\subsection{ULVZs and Partial Melting}
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{ulvz}
    \caption{Schematic of ULVZ detection locations and relative size, borrowed from \textcite{yuUltralowVelocityZone2018}. ULVZ extend 100's of km across the CMB surface, but seem to range from just 5--40~km thickness. However, ULVZs (or regions of ULVZs) thinner than 5~km thickness, or narrower than 100's of km, cannot be resolved by current seismic methods. \textit{a, b} and \textit{c} depict the relationship between ULVZs and deep mantle plumes, piles, and paleoslabs, respectively.}
    \label{fig:ulvz}

Ultra-low velocity zones (ULVZs) are small, thin \parencite[5--40~km thick;][]{garneroContinentsizedAnomalousZones2016} regions of thermochemically distinct material above the CMB that are frequently correlated with LLVPs. ULVZs may be the hottest deep mantle locations \parencite{yuUltralowVelocityZone2018}, and some studies suggest that they are the source location for deep mantle plumes \parencite{cottaarUnsuallyLargeULVZ2012}. ULVZs are characterized by strong reductions in seismic velocity, up to approximately 10\% in V\textsubscript{P} and 30\% in V\textsubscript{S}, and are often associated with increased density relative to the surrounding lowermost mantle \parencite{garneroContinentsizedAnomalousZones2016}. We expect these regions to be partially molten \parencite{yuUltralowVelocityZone2018}; evidence for partially molten (5--30\% melt) ULVZs comes primarily from the modelled 3:1 ratio in S to P wave velocities \parencite{berrymanSeismicVelocityDecrement2000}.
However, this observed velocity ratio could be explained by other features, such as chemical influence from the iron-rich outer core, or LPO. Aligned melt layers may result in SPO-induced seismic anisotropy, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:spo}, as modelled in \textcite{reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019}. If aligned melt is present, SPO from ULVZs could contribute to the strong anisotropy reported near LLVP centers, even though the interiors of LLVPs are expected to be weakly anisotropic \parencite{reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019,yuUltralowVelocityZone2018}. However, because ULVZs may contain both melt and compositionally distinct solid material, anisotropy in these regions cannot be uniquely attributed to melt-induced SPO without additional seismic and mineral physics constraints.

\section{Outstanding Challenges and Future Directions}

The observed distribution of D$''$ anisotropy is biased toward the Northern Hemisphere (see the relative prevalence of anisotropy in Figure~\ref{fig:methods}), which is most likely a result of uneven sampling because earthquake source locations are not evenly distributed, rather than a lack of anisotropy in the Southern Hemisphere \parencite{creasyConstrainingLowermostMantle2019}. On a smaller scale, outstanding uncertainties related to the lateral distribution of pPv (and other crucial lower mantle anisotropic phases) along the CMB limits our ability to interpret flow directions; numerical modelling provides important avenues for exploring such questions.

While methods for numerical modelling of LPO \parencite{kaminskiDRexProgramCalculation2004} have been around for over two decades, only recently have advances in computing power have enabled the simultaneous modeling of microscale processes and large-scale (regional or global) geodynamic flow models. These modern methods enable direct comparisons between hypothetical flow models and resulting seismic anisotropy. The ECOMAN package \parencite{faccendaECOMANOpensourcePackage2024} can be used as a post-processing routine to simulate the evolution of both LPO and SPO for both instantaneous and time-dependent models; it can also extrapolate the resultant elastic properties onto a tomographic grid and simulate synthetic SWS observations. \textcite{royLowermostMantleFlow2025} used ECOMAN, coupled with 3D numerical models of LLVPs in the lower mantle, to test the generation of strong radial anisotropy from deformation along the edges of LLVPs.

Alternatively, olivine LPO evolution in geodynamic models can now be modelled \textit{in situ} \parencite{vedavyasDRexNewTool2026,fratersImplementationUsabilityCrystal2021}, allowing for more realistic rheology-dependent viscosities in the model and co-evolution between anisotropic fabrics and mantle flow.

While these integrated methods are an important step for predicting upper mantle seismic anisotropy from olivine, further experimental work is needed to determine appropriate microphysical constraints, deformation mechanism maps, and the distribution of deep mantle minerals so that these methods can be applied effectively to the question of seismic anisotropy in the D$''$ layer. In combination with improved constraints from additional laboratory experiments of pPv, Br, and Fp, numerical modelling of realistic LPO development and flow is needed to evaluate the most likely spatial distribution of anisotropic phases in the lower mantle. This is a critical next step for understanding the structures and mantle flow driving D$''$ anisotropy.

\section{Conclusion}

A wide array of seismic techniques can be used to investigate seismic anisotropy in the lowermost regions of the mantle, providing important information about processes such as the subduction cycle, large-scale mantle dynamics, lower mantle geochemistry, and heat flux from the core. However, interpretations of anisotropy from these methods are currently limited both by their inherent physics, such as ray path-integration of splitting parameters, and poorly-constrained mineral physics of single-crystal elasticity and slip systems for deep mantle minerals. Further laboratory experiments exploring these parameters and recent advances in numerical modelling of LPO evolution stand out as clear paths forward to understand how D$''$ anisotropy relates to deep mantle structures, flow patterns, and the distribution of anisotropic minerals in the lowermost mantle.
