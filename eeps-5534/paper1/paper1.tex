\section*{Abstract}

% todo rework this, some pieces need to be pulled out into the intro

Seismic anisotropy is a phenomenon where seismic waves travel at different speeds depending on their direction of propagation \parencite{savageSeismicAnisotropyMantle1999}. It is commonly accepted that seismic anisotropy results from the lattice-preferred orientation (LPO) of anisotropic minerals in the mantle \parencite{hansenReviewMechanismsGenerating2021,babuskaSeismicAnisotropyEarth1991}, an idea originally proposed by \textcite{hessSeismicAnisotropyUppermost1964}. The formation of seismic anisotropy is largely controlled by the local deformation regime, which in turn depends on factors such as temperature, pressure, or the presence of water \parencite{hansenReviewMechanismsGenerating2021,skemerInfluenceWaterLPO2013}. As such, seismic anisotropy is a valuable tool for understanding deformational processes, structure, and physical conditions of the Earth's interior.
However, studies of seismic anisotropy often assume the lower mantle is isotropic \parencite{silverSeismicAnisotropyContinents1996,savageSeismicAnisotropyMantle1999}---an assumption based primarily on laboratory experiments of peridotites demonstrating LPO does not develop during diffusion creep \parencite{meadeLaboratorySeismologicalObservations1995,zhangLatticePreferredOrientation1995,karatoRoleRecrystallizationPreferred1988}, which is the dominant deformation regime expected under lower mantle conditions. However, convective flow in the lower 300~km of the mantle, known as the D$''$ layer after \textcite{bullenCompressibilityPressureHypothesisEarths1949}, has been shown to drive LPO development \parencite{karatoGeodynamicSignificanceSeismic2008}. Seismic anisotropy in the D$''$ layer has important implications for how we interpret observations of seismic anisotropy and our understanding of CMB and D$''$ dynamics.
% but recent research has revealed that this assumption may not be accurate \parencite{...}. In fact, several studies have reported the presence of anisotropy in the lower mantle, which has significant implications for our understanding of the Earth's interior.

We typically measure anisotropy using shear wave splitting (SWS), which can constrain the orientation and magnitude of anisotropy \parencite{chevrotShearWaveSplitting2004,longShearWaveSplitting2009}. New techniques for identifying and isolating deep mantle seismic anisotropy, such as differential splitting and waveform inversions \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}, can provide important insights into the dynamics of the lower mantle.
However, adequately constraining the source---particularly the depth---of anisotropy remains an outstanding challenge in geophysics \parencite{fischerDepthDistributionMantle1996,liuEstimationDepthAnisotropy2011}, because measurements of seismic anisotropy have poor vertical resolution because they are integrated along the full receiver-side ray-path of the wave \parencite{longShearWaveSplitting2009}.
Our understanding of seismic anisotropy along the core-mantle boundary (CMB) is further complicated by the relationship between anisotropy and large low-shear velocity provinces (LLSVPs) \parencite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017,wolfGlobalCompilationDeep2023}, ultra-low velocity zones (ULVZs) \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}, and the post-perovskite phase transition \parencite{peltierMantleDynamicsLayer2007}.
% The CMB is a region of intense geological activity, and the presence of anisotropy in this region may be related to the complex dynamics and interactions between the Earth's core and mantle.

Integrating multidisciplinary techniques is essential for advancing our understanding of seismic anisotropy in the deep mantle, as it allows us to relate the observed seismic properties to the underlying mineralogical and compositional characteristics of the Earth's interior. This paper will review our contemporary understanding anisotropy in the D$''$ along the CMB through a multidisciplinary lens combining seismology, mineral physics, and geochemistry to discuss how we measure and interpret deep mantle anisotropy, and outline outstanding questions and challenges.

\subsection{Source Mechanisms}

% todo add figure differentiating between LPO and SPO

Two distinct phenomena are responsible for generating seismic anisotropy: lattice-preferred orientation (LPO) and shape-preferred orientation (SPO). LPO, which is considered the dominant source of anisotropy \parencite{savageSeismicAnisotropyMantle1999}, forms in high-strain regions as material is deformed under shear stress imposed by mantle flow. Under simple shear, deformation via dislocation creep drives the preferential realignment of the crystal axes relative to the shear direction \parencite{kocksTextureAnisotropyPreferred2000}. However, this process is dependent on the relationship between the intrinsic properties of the deforming mineral phases and the local conditions \parencite{hansenReviewMechanismsGenerating2021,skemerInfluenceWaterLPO2013}.

While methods for numerical modelling of LPO \parencite{kaminskiDRexProgramCalculation2004} have been around for over two decades, only recently have advances in computing power have enabled the simultaneous modeling of microscale processes and large-scale (regional or global) geodynamic flow models. These modern methods enable direct comparisons between hypothetical flow models and resulting seismic anisotropy. The ECOMAN package \parencite{faccendaECOMANOpensourcePackage2024} can be used as a post-processing routine to simulate the evolution of both LPO and SPO for both instantaneous and time-dependent models; it can also extrapolate the resultant elastic properties onto a tomographic grid and simulate synthetic SWS observations.

Alternatively, olivine LPO evolution in geodynamic models can now be modelled \textit{in situ} \parencite{vedavyasDRexNewTool2026,fratersImplementationUsabilityCrystal2021}, allowing for more realistic rheology-dependent viscosities in the model and co-evolution between anisotropic fabrics and mantle flow. While these integrated methods are an important step for predicting upper mantle seismic anisotropy from olivine, further experimental work is needed to determine appropriate microphysical constraints, deformation maps, and the distribution of deep mantle minerals so that these exciting methods can be applied to the question of seismic anisotropy in the D'' layer.
