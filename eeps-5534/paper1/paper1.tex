Seismic anisotropy is a phenomenon in which seismic waves travel at different speeds depending on their propagation direction. It is commonly accepted that seismic anisotropy results primarily from the lattice-preferred orientation (LPO) formed during the deformation of anisotropic minerals in the mantle. As such, seismic anisotropy is a valuable tool for understanding deformational processes, structure, and the physical conditions of the Earth's interior.
Studies of seismic anisotropy often assume the bulk lower mantle is isotropic; however, there are several lines of evidence supporting seismic anisotropy from both LPO and shape-preferred orientation (SPO) in the lowermost 200--300~km of the mantle, known as the D$''$.
We typically measure anisotropy using shear wave splitting (SWS), which can constrain the orientation and magnitude of anisotropy. Techniques for identifying and isolating deep mantle seismic anisotropy, such as differential splitting and waveform inversions, can provide important insights into the dynamics of the lower mantle.
% However, adequately constraining the source---particularly the depth---of anisotropy remains an outstanding challenge in geophysics.
This paper will review our contemporary understanding of anisotropy in the D$''$ along the core-mantle boundary (CMB) through a multidisciplinary lens combining mineral physics, geochemistry, and seismology to discuss how we measure and interpret deep mantle anisotropy, and outline outstanding questions and challenges.

The D$''$ layer plays a fundamental role in the dynamics of the overlying mantle and the core below; it is the lower thermal boundary layer of the mantle convection system, it controls the heat flux out of the core that affects the geodynamo, and it is critical for understanding mantle plumes, slab stagnation, and global convection patterns \parencite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017,garneroNewParadigmEarths2004}. The D$''$ layer, Seismic anisotropy in the D$''$ layer is a crucial tool for understanding these lowermost mantle processes and structures \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}.
However, isolating and interpreting D$''$ anisotropy poses many challenges; our understanding of seismic anisotropy in this region along the core-mantle boundary (CMB) is complicated by the relationship between anisotropy and large low-shear-velocity (LLVPs) \parencite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017,wolfGlobalCompilationDeep2023}, ultra-low velocity zones (ULVZs) \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}, and the distribution and contributions to anisotropy of the dominant mineral phases (post-perovskite, ferropericlase, and bridgmanite) \parencite{peltierMantleDynamicsLayer2007}.

D$''$ anisotropy is highly dependent on the distribution of the dominant lower mantle minerals and their relative anisotropies. \textcite{hirose205MineralogyDeep2015} suggest the relative abundance of the primary minerals is about \textasciitilde30\% \ce{(Mg, Fe)O} (ferropericlase; Fp) and \textasciitilde70\% \ce{(Mg, Fe)SiO3}, "…which may be present as bridgmanite [Br] in hotter regions and post-perovskite [pPv] in colder ones" \parencite{hirose205MineralogyDeep2015}. Critically, improvements in experimental technologies and techniques in the early 2000s made it possible to experimentally isolate the pPv phase, which has a strong intrinsic elastic anisotropy and may be the dominant phase in the anisotropic regions of D$''$  \parencite{oganovTheoreticalExperimentalEvidence2004,murakamiPostPerovskitePhaseTransition2004}. Phase transitions to pPv structure at lowermost mantle P-T conditions are reported for many compositions \parencite{hirosePostperovskitePhaseTransition2006}.
Generally speaking, two distinct phenomena are responsible for generating seismic anisotropy: lattice-preferred orientation (LPO) and shape-preferred orientation (SPO). LPO, which is considered the dominant source of anisotropy \parencite{savageSeismicAnisotropyMantle1999}, forms in high-strain regions as material is deformed under shear stress imposed by mantle flow. Under simple shear, deformation via dislocation creep drives the preferential realignment of the crystal axes relative to the shear direction \parencite{kocksTextureAnisotropyPreferred2000}. SPO may be an alternative or complementary explanation for D$''$ anisotropy, but it is difficult to differentiate its effect \parencite{reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019}.

% Different seismological techniques are used to infer D$''$ anisotropy, and each are able to resolve different anisotropic geometries and spatial distributions (see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}).
A variety of body wave methods are used to detect deep mantle anisotropy. Methods for D$''$ anisotropy typically rely on polarization-dependent shear waves, rather than compressional (P) waves. Each approach is sensitive to different aspects of D$''$ anisotropic geometry (i.e., radial vs. azimuthal) \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}, and when used in combination, can provide a more holistic picture of D$''$ deformation and potential structures \parencite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017}.

Integrating these multidisciplinary techniques is key to advance our understanding of seismic anisotropy in the deep mantle, as it allows us to relate the underlying structural and compositional characteristics of the Earth's interior to the observed seismic properties.

\section{Mechanisms and Mineral Physics}

LPO development in the lowermost mantle depends on the relationship between the intrinsic properties of the deforming mineral phase and the local conditions, which controls slip system activation \parencite{hansenReviewMechanismsGenerating2021,carrezImplicationsPlasticFlow2007,romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017}.
The dominant slip system in pPv is not well-constrained \parencite{romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017,carrezImplicationsPlasticFlow2007,reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019}. Experimental and computational studies propose different deformation mechanisms, including slip on the (001), (010) \parencite[strong \ce{CaIrO3} pPv LPO][]{carrezImplicationsPlasticFlow2007}, and \{110\} planes \parencite[\ce{MgGeO3} pPv analogue][]{carrezImplicationsPlasticFlow2007}. Because each proposed slip system produces a distinct LPO, the resulting radial and azimuthal anisotropy also differs. Recent models of LPO evolution in pPv (\textasciitilde70\%) and Fp (\textasciitilde30\%) for both CMB-parallel flow and upwelling/downwelling flow by \textcite{tommasiDeformationCrystalPreferred2018} produce both radial and azimuthal anisotropy components.

In particular, LPO developed in Fp within cold downwelling regions may produce measurable SWS, although its deformation behavior and slip-system activity remain poorly constrained. However, it is less abundant than pPv. The relative distribution of both phases in different regions of the lowermost mantle may control

Potential SPO anisotropic sources include alignment of partial melt lenses of pPv, compositional heterogeneity (layering), or distinct mineral domains, which can produce seismic anisotropy if the aligned structures have a sufficient elastic contrast and consistent orientation.

Interpreting anisotropy in the lowermost mantle is complicated because the distribution of these constituents is poorly constrained; pPv is unlikely to be ubiquitous across the D$''$ region. Moreover, because multiple mechanisms can produce similar radial or azimuthal anisotropy signatures, seismic observations alone rarely uniquely identify the underlying mineral fabric, deformation mechanism, or flow geometry. Interpreting D$''$ anisotropy therefore requires combining seismic observations with mineral-physics constraints and models of lowermost mantle flow.

\section{Methods for Detecting Deep Mantle Anisotropy}
% As such, I will review the most common S wave methods below.

\subsection{Differential SV-SH travel times}

Differential SV-SH travel times from teleseismic S, ScS, and S\textsubscript{diff} phases are among the most common methods for interrogating anisotropy in D$''$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}a) \parencite{nowackiNewAdvancesUsing2011,wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}. Differential SH-SV travel-time analysis decomposes a single phase into its SH and SV polarizations, where SV particle motion lies within the great-circle plane containing the ray path and receiver, and SH particle motion is orthogonal to that plane. In a radially anisotropic medium, these two polarizations propagate at different speeds. Radial anisotropy is commonly quantified as

\begin{equation}
    \xi = \left(\frac{V_{SH}}{V_{SV}}\right)^2 = \frac{N}{L}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where $N$ and $L$ are the Love parameters \parencite{skemerInferringUppermantleFlow2016}. The relative velocities of the two polarizations provide information about the orientation of the anisotropic symmetry axis: $\xi > 1$ indicates a vertically transversely isotropic (VTI) fabric with a vertical symmetry axis, and $\xi < 1$ indicates a horizontally transversely isotropic (HTI) fabric with a horizontal symmetry axis. $\xi = 1$ indicates no radial anisotropy for the sampled propagation direction, although it does not preclude azimuthal anisotropy or a tilted transverse isotropy (TTI). D$''$ anisotropy is frequently interpreted as being radially anisotropic, with vertical fabrics \parencite{wysessionLateralVariationsCompressional1999,kendallConstraintsSeismicAnisotropy1996}. See Section~\ref{sec:interp} for further discussion of the causes of these theorized vertical fabrics.

\subsection{Differential splitting techniques}

When shear waves enter an anisotropic medium, they are split into two orthogonally polarized quasi-shear wave components that travel along the fast and slow axes of the material \parencite{silverShearWaveSplitting1991,vecseyShearwaveSplittingMeasurements2008}. The delay time ($\delta t$) between the arrival of the fast and slow components at the receiver, and the fast polarization direction ($\phi$, measured from north; or $\phi'$, in the ray frame) parameters are used to describe this shear wave splitting (SWS) phenomena (see Figure~\ref{fig:sks-summary}) \parencite{vinnikAzimuthalAnisotropyLithosphere1989,silverShearWaveSplitting1991}.

The components are travelling along the fast (blue) and slow (red) axes defined by the elastic properties (e.g., LPO, SPO; here unspecified) of the D$''$ layer. While not explicitly depicted in the figure, the fast direction ($\phi$) is typically measured from north. Borrowed from \textcite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}.}
    \label{fig:sks-summary}
\end{figure}
These differential splitting techniques primarily identify azimuthal anisotropy, which assumes a horizontal symmetry axis such that velocities vary as a function of the horizontal direction \parencite{nowackiNewAdvancesUsing2011,longShearWaveSplitting2009}. Azimuthal anisotropy can be used to distinguish horizontal flow patterns. In the Earth, SWS is commonly observed using XKS phases (SKS, SKKS, S3KS, and PKS), which undergo P-to-S conversion at the CMB before propagating upward through anisotropic mantle layers. Because this conversion produces an initially radially polarized S wave, any splitting accumulated on the source side of the path is removed; therefore, anisotropy along the receiver-side path transfers energy from the radial to the transverse component \parencite{schulte-pelkumSynthesisSeismicAnisotropy2003,silverShearWaveSplitting1991,longShearWaveSplitting2009}.
However, because the SWS effect is integrated over the whole ray path, determining where at depth anisotropy originates is a challenge.

To overcome this issue, some studies have used differential splitting between XKS phases \parencite[e.g., SKS--SKKS;][]{niuSeismicAnisotropyLower2004,vanacoreCharacterizationGalapagosIslands2011,royUpperLowerMantle2014} to isolate the anisotropy in the deep mantle (see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}c). This method is based on the assumption that the ray paths of the phases are distinct in the D$''$ layer but nearly overlapping in the anisotropic upper mantle, therefore, large differences in the observed SWS for each phase can be attributed to anisotropy in the lowermost mantle \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024,nowackiNewAdvancesUsing2011,wolfGlobalCompilationDeep2023}. Note, however, that differences in upper mantle structure may account for small changes in the SKS-SKKS differential \parencite{linFullwaveEffectsShear2014}. Furthermore, evidence for SKS-SKKS splitting in D$''$ is rare, which is likely a result of finite-frequency effects; based on anisotropic sensitivity kernels, SKS/SKKS have a strong sensitivity kernel for a 50--200~km conic region of the upper mantle, but a weak sensitivity distributed over a 600--1000~km wide zone in the lowermost mantle \parencite{tommasiDeformationCrystalPreferred2018,sieminskiFiniteFrequencySKSSplitting2008,favierSensitivityKernelsShear2003}. Taking their limitations into account, XKS differential splitting methods are still useful for interpreting flow and anisotropic structure along LLVPs, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:african-LLVP} \parencite{reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019}.

Non-XKS phases can also be used; for instance, S-ScS differential splitting (see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}d) is a common approach based on the fact that only the ScS phase passes through the D$''$ layer, but both phases have similar ray paths in the upper mantle on both the source and receiver sides \parencite{wookeyLowermostMantleAnisotropy2005}. However, it has been shown the S-ScS differential splitting may produce apparent splitting if the phase shift of the radial component due to the CMB reflection is not taken into account \parencite{wolfConstrainingDeepMantle2022}.


\subsection{Polarity Analysis}

% Measurements of polarities of S phases that turn in the lowermost mantle (S, SdS, PdP)

Anomalous polarities of S waves reflecting off the CMB can be used to infer anisotropic structures, generally with a tilted symmetry axis, relative to vertical (TTI) \parencite{garneroVariableAzimuthalAnisotropy2004}. P and S waves reflecting off the D$''$ (SdS, PdP; see Figure~\ref{fig:methods}b) can also be used in polarity analysis using array processing techniques such as beamforming \parencite{piscontiDiscriminatingCausesAnisotropy2019,piscontiMineralogyFabricDeformation2023}. The reflection polarities are compared to other seismic phases (S/ScS and P/PcP), and can be used to differentiate between different causes for the observed anisotropy, such as phase transitions (e.g., the perovskite to post-perovskite phase transition), mineral texture or thermal anomalies \parencite{thomasAnisotropyCausePolarity2011}. The polarities are best utilized as an additional constraint for deep mantle anisotropy, rather than an independent observation \parencite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}. \textcite{piscontiDiscriminatingCausesAnisotropy2019,piscontiMineralogyFabricDeformation2023} describe several important best practices for polarity analysis, which are well-summarized in \textcite{wolfAdvancesMappingLowermost2024}.

\section{Current Interpretations of Deep Mantle Anisotropy}
\label{sec:interp}

\subsection{LLVPs and Mantle Flow}

Along the CMB, two large low-shear-velocity provinces (LLVPs) underlie Africa and the Pacific. Possible explanations include the accumulation of subducted paleoslab material, primordial reservoirs, and thermochemical piles in which thermal buoyancy partly offsets a compositional density increase \parencite{lithgow-bertelloniDynamicsCenozoicMesozoic1998,davailleDeflatingLLSVPsBundles2020,royLowermostMantleFlow2025}. Seismic, geochemical, and mineral physics constraints indicate that LLVPs are hotter and chemically distinct from the surrounding lowermost mantle, and that their density is higher, suggesting they are dynamically stable and long-lived \parencite{garneroContinentsizedAnomalousZones2016}. An active interpretation of LLVP origin is based on the idea that thermochemical heterogeneity generates buoyancy-driven vertical flow \parencite{davailleSimultaneousGenerationHotspots1999}; whereas the passive interpretation proposes that dense, chemically-distinct material is shaped by large-scale mantle convection, with implications for slab accumulation, CMB heat flux, and upper-mantle dynamics \parencite{mcnamaraThermochemicalStructuresAfrica2005,reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019}.

Regional seismic studies commonly report strong radial anisotropy (\textasciitilde4\%) near LLVP margins \parencite{reissLowermostMantleAnisotropy2019,cottaarObservationsChangingAnisotropy2013,wangMappingGeometryGeographic2004}. This has been interpreted as evidence for vertical flow, or for ambient mantle flow deflected upward along the steep sides of the provinces, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:african-LLVP} \parencite{royLowermostMantleFlow2025,romanowiczAnisotropyDeepEarth2017}. By contrast, anisotropy appears weaker and more heterogeneous in LLVP interiors, although it is not necessarily absent \parencite{cottaarObservationsChangingAnisotropy2013}. Thus, LLVP margins may drive deformation and upwelling, but their interiors may be less deformed (lower anisotropy) or less well resolved seismically.

While methods for numerical modelling of LPO \parencite{kaminskiDRexProgramCalculation2004} have been around for over two decades, only recently have advances in computing power have enabled the simultaneous modeling of microscale processes and large-scale (regional or global) geodynamic flow models. These modern methods enable direct comparisons between hypothetical flow models and resulting seismic anisotropy. The ECOMAN package \parencite{faccendaECOMANOpensourcePackage2024} can be used as a post-processing routine to simulate the evolution of both LPO and SPO for both instantaneous and time-dependent models; it can also extrapolate the resultant elastic properties onto a tomographic grid and simulate synthetic SWS observations. \textcite{royLowermostMantleFlow2025} used ECOMAN, coupled with 3D numerical models of LLVPs in the lower mantle, to test the generation of strong radial anisotropy from deformation along the edges of LLVPs.

Alternatively, olivine LPO evolution in geodynamic models can now be modelled \textit{in situ} \parencite{vedavyasDRexNewTool2026,fratersImplementationUsabilityCrystal2021}, allowing for more realistic rheology-dependent viscosities in the model and co-evolution between anisotropic fabrics and mantle flow.
